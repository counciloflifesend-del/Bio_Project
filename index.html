00

Abstract

Cellular respiration is the process by which a cell generates ATP to use in cellular processes. It is a well known process that is vital to our existence. Humanity has been aware of its existence for a long time, though recently discoveries have allowed us to paint a much clearer picture of what actually happens inside every single one of the trillions upon trillions of living cells that make up our body. And that is what this research paper is about, unlocking the secret behind cellular respiration through a few selected new discoveries. Specifically, we looked at recent developments in respiratory supercomplexes, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and the oxygen-sensing mechanisms whose discovery was part of the 2019 Nobel Prize. These taken together paints a much clearer picture of how the cell regulates and reacts to a change in oxygen levels, something that is vital to the aerobic phase of Cellular Respiration. Through reviewing and comparing published literature, it is clear to us that humanity has made significant advances in this field, we now understand cellular respiration and the processes closely connected to it like never before, but it is still not enough.

Key words that are critical to our paper include cellular respiration, electron transport chain (ETC), oxidative phosphorylation, mitochondrial supercomplexes, respirasome, oxygen sensing, hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF), and reactive oxygen species (ROS).

01

Introduction

Almost every person has heard of the term “cellular respiration” at least once in their lives. And all of them have definitely experienced it (they wouldn’t be alive if they didn’t). Indeed, cellular respiration is so important that its existence was discovered centuries ago. The process of respiration in itself was known to humanity…well few people can say exactly when it was discovered. Homo sapiens and Neanderthals must have known that if you squeeze someone’s neck for an extensive period of time, that person will die. And we have considerable evidence that suggests that Ancient hominins knew being completely submerged in water for an hour is not the optimal state of being. They did not understand the entire cellular process nearly as well as we did however. This is because we, in 2026, know the proceedings better than we had a million years ago, better than we had a thousand year ago, better than we had a century ago, and even surprisingly better than we had less than two decades ago. So exactly what are the things humanity has discovered in this amount of time? And how do they affect our understanding of this vital cellular process? To understand these discoveries better, we first need to understand the basic processes. Cellular respiration is the process by which cells break down glucose sugar, and use it to generate ATP, which is the energy cells use for numerous cellular activities. Glucose is first split, then the pieces, if oxygen is present, go through a cycle that provides high energy electrons, and finally those electrons are used to create a chemical gradient and drive the production of ATP through ATP Synthase.

02

Literature Review

Research by Dunn and Grider (2020) shows triphosphate (ATP) as the main energy source created through respiration while Gourlay (2020) describes more general ways the mitochondria work. However even though these studies explain energy production inside the cell they offer a picture that misses details about how things are arranged inside the inner mitochondrial membrane.

To fill this gap new studies have looked at the structure of the membrane. Each study has its own focus. A 2016 study from Protein & Cell uses imaging to show that proteins involved in the electron transport chain connect into organized groups called "respirasomes" to work better(Guo et al., 2016). Another study by Skulachev et al. (2023) Takes a view saying that arranging these parts helps protect the cell by controlling harmful molecules and slowing down aging. Looking at these studies shows that scientists are moving away from ideas about proteins floating around to a more organized and active membrane system.

Looking at the picture the 2019 Nobel Prize research by Kaelin, Ratcliffe and Semenza studies how cells detect oxygen the final part of the electron transport chain and how this leads to changes in genes, through the HIF pathway. Before most studies looked at how cells make energy but the Nobel-winning work shows how these systems help the whole body survive. These studies link the way the membrane is built to how the body stays balanced showing that cellular respiration is an varied system.

High‑resolution imaging gives proof of respirasomes and thorough reviews like Skulachev et al. (2023) bring together a lot of data about oxidative stress. The main problem is that we still cannot fully turn these results into living organisms. Even though classic books such as Dunn and Grider (2020) and Gourlay (2020) set basic rules for cellular energy, research shows a big gap between these membrane setups and real treatments. The clear visual proof of respirasomes shows how these tiny machines work inside cells. Oxidative stress remains a challenge in many diseases. Cellular energy rules how cells behave and how they survive.

Future studies must figure out how breaking apart these organized complexes changes the way diseases get worse. The way we interrupt respirasomes or change stress levels may shift disease progression and affect how cells respond to damage.

Finally putting together all these ways of looking—structure studies and watching how the body senses oxygen—highlights a shift in how we see cell biology. Stage 3 cellular respiration is no longer a single chemical route; it is now a layered system. These linked discoveries show that mitochondrial organization directly controls how long cells live and how the whole body adapts to the environment. Mitochondrial organization is the backbone of energy and dictates both longevity and adaptation in the body.

03

Synthesis

Indeed, the primary function of cellular respiration, as we learned in school, is to provide energy. The place where it happens, the mitochondria, is depicted as the energy house for our bodies, where glucose enter it and be broke down, releasing electrons that motivate the ETC (electron transport chain), creating a concentration gradient, and thereby producing ATP, which is the energy source of our body. According to Dunn & Grider (2020), There’s approximately 100-150 moles of ATP been used by our body to move ions, contract muscles and fire nerve impulses. If that number is not as sensible, Skulachev et.al (2023) described the consumption from another perspective, that around 50 kilograms of ATP is being used and made in human’s bodies whithin a day.

However, cellular resipiration is more facinating than what we learned from the textbook. Skulachev et al. (2023) listed six functions of respiration: making ATP, consuming fuel, powering membrane transport, generating heat, clearing oxygen to prevent damage, and producing reactive oxygen species (ROS) as signals. The last one changes the picture. They treat ROS as a signal rather than just damage, and therefore respiration becomes a control problem: the proton gradient has to be high enough for ATP synthesis and low enough to keep ROS under control. It help to address the problem that ROS will leak from the ETC in complex I and III, and the chance of the leakage is increasing as the concentration is increasing.

Some of the claims are stable while some are not. Everyone ackknowledge that electron transfer is coupled to proton pumping and that the gradient drives ATP synthesis, with 2.7 H+ per ATP as the accepted cost in animal mitochondria (Gourlay, 2020; Skulachev et al., 2023). The difference is in The disagreements start as soon as you ask how the chain is organized. The fluid model says the complexes drift independently; the solid model says they are locked into supercomplexes; the plastic model says assembly shifts with the cell's needs (Guo et al., 2016). Mice missing SCAF1 have no III–IV supercomplexes but still seems to be normal, and no one has provide reasons about why these supercomplexes produce less ROS. Aging is also still under debate. Skulachev et al. (2023) describe it as a chronic phenoptosis, a programmed decline driven in part by losing those protective enzymes and by weaker cardiolipin repair. they acknowledge that isolated mitochondria from old animals often make no extra ROS. Even the numbers are contested, since there is still no agreed H+/O2 ratio; it can be 12 or 20 depending on the pathway (Gourlay, 2020). The 2019 Nobel Prize completed the picture at the level of the whole body, showing how low oxygen lets HIF-1α escape destruction by the VHL protein and switch on genes for red blood cells, blood vessels and metabolism (The Nobel Assembly at Karolinska Institutet, 2019).

There are still a lot of remaining questions exsists according to the literature: What turns cardiolipin damage into mitophagy in one cell and apoptosis in another? Do the lost enzymes actually cause aging, or just follow it, and why do long-lived mole rats and bats keep them? Why has no human trial shown that mitochondria-targeted antioxidants slow aging, when they reliably rescue mice from shock? And how many of the disagreements above are really about biology rather than about different labs measuring respiration differently (Gourlay, 2020)?

From the research, there is a more holistic view of the cellular repiration, that respiration is regulated at several levels at once: how supercomplexes assemble, how mitochondria signal distress through ROS and cardiolipin, and how the body adjusts oxygen delivery through HIF. The six functions are not rival definitions. They are one system, and ROS is how that system keeps an eye on itself.

04

Conclusion

In summary, the third stage of cell respiration is more dynamic than previously thought. The newly discovered structural research reveals specific ways protein complexes work together for efficiency and reduce cellular stress. Also, the Nobel Prize research explains processes cells check oxygen to adapt to environmental changes. In the future, research should combine cellular structure with their uncovered findings to better understand diseases and metabolic health. The more we discover, the more truth we unveil, the more knowledge we seem to know we lack. Despite the recent breakthroughs, there are many mysteries left for a new generation of people, like us, to solve.
